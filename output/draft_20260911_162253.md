## Introduction, Background & Conceptual Framework

SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND

5.1 Context and Strategic Importance

DanChurchAid Kenya, with TUPADO (Kenya), ROG and ICPDO (South Sudan) and ECC (Ethiopia), is implementing the European Union-funded project Strengthening Integrated Peace, Resilience and Disaster Risk Reduction for Cross-border Communities in the border regions of South Omo/Ethiopia, Turkana/Kenya and Eastern Equatoria/South Sudan (SPREAD: Peace & Resilience). The 42-month project, now in its third year and moving toward its concluding phase, was deliberately designed as an integrated programme combining peacebuilding, livelihoods, climate resilience and Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) rather than delivering them as parallel, siloed activities. Working with pastoralists/agro-pastoralists, fisherfolk, women- and youth-led CSOs, traditional leaders, local/national authorities, the private sector and IGAD across the Karamoja Cluster, SPREAD has introduced Community Action Groups (CAGs), Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLAs), micro-grants and early warning systems alongside pre-existing structures such as market committees, peace committees and savings groups. What is now needed is a rigorous, evidence-based account of what this integration has meant operationally and when it has added value beyond the sum of its parts. 5.2 Purpose and Objectives

The purpose of Learning Paper 2 is to establish a shared, evidence-based understanding of what integration means and delivers in the SPREAD context, and to translate this into actionable guidance for future programming and financing models, including anticipatory action. The six specific objectives are: to establish a shared operational definition of integration as applied in SPREAD design and inception, documenting how sequencing and coordination between peacebuilding, livelihoods, climate resilience and DRR components were implemented in practice; to document challenges and trade-offs of integrating thematic activities at local level, linked to reducing pressure on natural resources through diversified income opportunities; to use community case studies of stronger and weaker integration to show how interventions were experienced as one package and why integrated programming worked better in some locations; to assess how communities relate to SPREAD-introduced structures alongside pre-existing structures and capture preferences for future integration; to produce a detailed learning paper and a concise policy brief for donors, IGAD and governments on which components should be integrated, at what level, and which can simply be coordinated; and to develop a policy brief on how SPREAD's integrated approach contributes to local and regional stability and human security, with actionable lessons for donors and policymakers including the EU and Denmark. 5.3 Our Interpretation of the Assignment

We read this assignment not as an evaluation but as applied comparative learning: the decisive analytical task is to convert SPREAD's existing evidence base, including the baseline report (2024), the Mid-Term Review report (2025) and the Year 3/MTR reflection workshop outputs, into a defensible verdict on the integration versus coordination distinction, tested through community case studies of stronger and weaker integration across all three borderlands. Two implications follow that the ToR implies but does not spell out. First, the sequence of milestones means that once the inception report and tools are approved on 13 October 2026, only about two and a half weeks remain for tri-country field data collection before 31 October; the case-study protocol, harmonised tools and enumerator training must therefore be substantively drafted from the desk review before inception approval, with partner coordination through TUPADO, ICPDO, ROG and ECC mobilised in parallel rather than sequentially. Second, because "integration" has no agreed operational definition at the outset, the working definition produced from the desk review must be validated with the Consortium Management Unit (CMU) and thematic leads at the inception briefing before it is used as the yardstick for case-study selection; otherwise the comparative findings would rest on a contested benchmark. This interpretation directly targets the two most heavily weighted technical criteria, understanding of the task (15 points) and clarity and appropriateness of methodology, sampling criteria and sample size (20 points). 5.4 Key Evaluation Questions

We adopt the ToR's six proposed key learning questions as the analytical spine, to be finalised jointly with DCA at inception:

1. What does 'integration' mean operationally in the SPREAD context, and which sequencing approaches, for example peace preceding livelihood investment, economic cooperation opening space for dialogue, or natural-resource agreements preceding irrigation or agricultural investment, work best in which contexts? 2. What challenges and trade-offs arise from integrating peace and resilience at community level, and how does this relate to reducing pressure on natural resources through diversified income opportunities? 3. How have SPREAD structures (e.g. CMDRR, CAGs, observatory platforms) been perceived by communities and government, and how do they complement pre-existing structures such as market committees, peace committees and savings groups? 5. Which components of integrated peace, livelihoods, climate resilience and DRR programming genuinely need to be integrated, at what level, and which can simply be coordinated, and what does this imply for future funding models, including anticipatory action? 6. How is the SPREAD integrated peace-resilience approach positively contributing to stability and human security locally and regionally, and what can policymakers learn from it? 5.5 Deliverables

SECTION 2: CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

The analytical lens for Learning Paper 2 is a qualitative comparative case-study framework organised around the integration versus coordination distinction that anchors the ToR's final recommendations, embedded within the Humanitarian-Development-Peace nexus. Rather than asking whether SPREAD achieved results, the framework asks under what conditions combining peacebuilding, livelihoods, climate resilience and DRR components produced added value beyond the sum of its parts, and under what conditions coordination between separately delivered components would have sufficed. This is precisely the question the ToR poses, and it demands a design built for cross-case comparison, not a standard results assessment. The framework operates on three levels. At the definitional level, we construct a working operational definition of integration from the SPREAD baseline report (2024), the Mid-Term Review report (2025), Year 3/MTR reflection workshop outputs and DCA's own guidance, including ConflictPrevention_ActionGuide2024, SocialCohesion_ActionGuide2024, the Social Cohesion Index tool - DCA 26.10.22.xlsx, DCA's resilience building framework and the Market Systems Development and Financial Inclusion guidance. This definition, validated with the CMU and thematic leads at inception, specifies observable markers of integration in design, sequencing, staffing, targeting and community structures, so that stronger and weaker integration sites can be selected against explicit criteria rather than reputation. At the explanatory level, we apply a contribution logic built on SPREAD's existing Outcome Harvesting practice: harvested outcomes from CAGs, VSLAs, micro-grants, early warning systems, CMDRR structures and observatory platforms are traced backward to the sequencing pathways the ToR itself hypothesises, such as peace preceding livelihood investment, economic cooperation opening space for dialogue, and natural-resource agreements preceding irrigation or agricultural investment. Cross-case comparison across South Omo, Turkana and Eastern Equatoria then explains why integration worked better in some locations than others, with disaggregation by sex, age and disability and GESI and conflict-sensitive framing applied throughout. At the policy level, findings are mapped from community-level evidence to human security and stability outcomes, translating what pastoralists/agro-pastoralists, fisherfolk, traders in formal and informal cross-border trade (ICBT) and traditional leaders experience into the language of funding and programming models, including anticipatory action, that donors, IGAD, EU and Danish policymakers can act on.

## Technical Methodology

METHODOLOGY

1. Overall Approach

Cortech Consulting Group proposes a mixed-methods, comparative case-study design that treats Learning Paper 2 not as a routine end-of-project evaluation but as a structured inquiry into a single decidable question: which components of peacebuilding, livelihoods, climate resilience and Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) programming in SPREAD genuinely need to be integrated, at what level, and which can simply be coordinated. The design responds directly to the six specific objectives and six key learning questions in the Terms of Reference, and to the ToR's own sequencing hypotheses: peace preceding livelihood investment, economic cooperation opening space for dialogue, and natural-resource agreements preceding irrigation or agricultural investment. Each hypothesis will be tested against community-level evidence from the three SPREAD border regions of South Omo/Ethiopia, Turkana/Kenya and Eastern Equatoria/South Sudan within the Karamoja Cluster. The analytical engine of the design is the deliberate pairing of stronger and weaker examples of integration, as the ToR requires under objective 3. By purposively selecting community case studies where interventions were experienced as one package and cases where they were not, and holding the comparison across all three countries, the analysis can explain why integrated programming worked better in some locations than others rather than merely describing that it did. This comparison is anchored in SPREAD's existing Outcome Harvesting practice: harvested outcomes from the SPREAD baseline report (2024), the Mid-Term Review report (2025) and the Year 3/MTR reflection workshop outputs form the starting evidence, which fieldwork then substantiates, challenges and extends. The approach is locally led and conflict-sensitive throughout. Data collection will be coordinated through the national partner teams, TUPADO (Kenya), ICPDO and ROG (South Sudan) and ECC (Ethiopia), using harmonised tools to ensure comparability across countries, as the cross-border coordination mechanism in the ToR specifies. GESI is embedded by design: sampling deliberately covers women- and youth-led CSOs and mixed markets, and all data are disaggregated by sex, age and disability. This section directly addresses evaluation criteria 4, 5, 6 and 7 (understanding of the task, ToR addressed in sufficient detail, clarity and appropriateness of methodology and sampling, and logical sequencing), which together carry 70 of the 100 technical points. 2. Phase-by-Phase Breakdown

Phase 1, Inception (contract signature to 13 October 2026). The lead researcher conducts a structured desk review of the SPREAD baseline report (2024), the Mid-Term Review report (2025), Year 3/MTR reflection workshop outputs, interim reports, ConflictPrevention_ActionGuide2024, SocialCohesion_ActionGuide2024, the Social Cohesion Index tool - DCA 26.10.22.xlsx, DCA's resilience building framework, the Market Systems Development and Financial Inclusion guidance, community-level data on CAGs, VSLAs, micro-grants, market committees, farmers and farmer groups and early warning systems, and comparable integrated-programming studies from other Karamoja Cluster actors. The desk review produces a working operational definition of integration in the SPREAD context and a documented account of how sequencing and coordination between components were designed at inception (objective 1). In consultation with SPREAD technical and MEAL staff, we develop the case-study selection criteria, the community case-study protocol, KII and FGD guides and participatory ranking instruments. All tools are scripted in KoboToolbox with Kenyan, Ethiopian and South Sudanese versions harmonised for cross-country comparability. The inception report justifies the final choice of methods and sample, as the ToR expressly requires, and includes ethical and risk mitigation measures. An inception briefing with the SPREAD CMU and thematic leads closes the phase and constitutes the approval gate before any fieldwork begins. Phase 2, Field Data Collection (14 to 31 October 2026). Cortech recruits and trains local research assistants and enumerators in each country, remunerated at reasonable market rates in line with the Code of Conduct for Contractors, with introductions and in-country coordination facilitated by TUPADO, ICPDO, ROG and ECC. Sampling spans peacebuilding and resilience structures respectively, and the livestock, fishing, farming and formal and informal cross-border trade (ICBT) livelihood groups, across all three countries, including both women/youth-led and mixed markets. Respondent categories include community members, CAG and VSLA members, micro-grant recipients, traditional leaders, market committees, peace committees, savings groups and local authorities, so that SPREAD-introduced structures can be assessed alongside pre-existing ones (objective 4 and learning question 4 on CMDRR, CAGs and observatory platforms). Phase 3, Analysis (1 to 20 November 2026). Quantitative data exported from KoboToolbox are cleaned and analysed in SPSS, disaggregated by sex, age and disability, and triangulated against the SPREAD baseline and MTR values. Qualitative transcripts are coded in NVivo using a framework built around the six learning questions, with cross-case matrices comparing stronger and weaker integration sites. Substantiated outcomes are mapped through Outcome Harvesting logic to the integration versus coordination distinction. A dedicated review session with the SPREAD team, as Task 4 requires, is used to deepen and quality-check the interpretation of findings before drafting. Phase 4, Reporting and Validation (21 November to 15 December 2026). Draft learning paper and draft policy brief submitted by 21 November and discussed with the Consortium Management Unit (CMU) and relevant thematic leads; the single cross-border validation workshop with Consortium members, IGAD and other key stakeholders held by 3 December; final learning paper and both policy briefs, incorporating validation feedback, submitted by 15 December 2026. 3. Quality is managed at four points. Second, daily server-side data checks during the 14 to 31 October window flag outliers, duplicates and incomplete records for same-day correction while teams remain in the field. Third, all findings are triangulated across at least two sources: field data against the SPREAD baseline (2024) and MTR (2025), qualitative against quantitative, and community accounts against partner and authority perspectives. Fourth, the review session with the SPREAD team and the cross-border validation workshop function as substantive quality gates, ensuring findings are recognisable and defensible to those closest to the programme before finalisation. 5. Ethical Considerations

All participation is by informed consent, administered in the respondent's preferred language, with no incentives that could distort responses. Conflict-sensitive framing governs tool design, site scheduling and reporting: questions on cross-border conflict and resource competition are phrased to avoid attributing blame to identifiable groups, and enumerator deployment is agreed with TUPADO, ICPDO, ROG and ECC to respect local dynamics. Data are anonymised at cleaning, stored on access-controlled servers, and handled in line with Cortech's child safeguarding and PSEA policies and DCA's data protection requirements; all data and case-study files become the property of the Contracting Authority. GESI sensitivity is operationalised through sex-segregated FGDs where appropriate, deliberate inclusion of persons with disabilities in sampling, and disaggregated reporting throughout, so that the preferences of women, youth and marginalised community members are visible in the final recommendations.

Learning Paper 2 does not require a probability sample or statistical precision level; the assignment is a learning study, not a representative survey. Selection will instead be purposive, applying case-study selection criteria co-developed with SPREAD technical and MEAL staff to identify stronger and weaker examples of integration across South Omo/Ethiopia, Turkana/Kenya and Eastern Equatoria/South Sudan. KII and FGD participants will be selected deliberately to span peacebuilding and resilience structures, livestock, fishing, farming, and formal and informal cross-border trade (ICBT), including women/youth-led and mixed markets, CAG and VSLA members, traditional leaders and local/national authorities, so that every learning question can be answered from more than one respondent category in each country. The final selection logic and sample composition will be justified in the inception report, and any limitations explained in the final report, as the ToR requires and as the 20-point methodology and sampling criterion demands. SECTION 2. DATA ANALYSIS PLAN

Quantitative data arising from the participatory ranking exercises, the Social Cohesion Index tool - DCA 26.10.22.xlsx where applied, and structured elements of the community case-study protocol will be exported from KoboToolbox, cleaned, coded and entered into SPSS. Analysis will be primarily descriptive: frequencies, cross-tabulations and mean rank scores comparing community preferences for greater integration of conflict prevention and disaster risk reduction groups across the three countries, across livelihood groups (pastoralists/agro-pastoralists, fisherfolk, farmers, ICBT traders) and across stronger and weaker integration sites. All quantitative outputs will be disaggregated by sex, age and disability. Where the SPREAD baseline report (2024) and Mid-Term Review report (2025) contain comparable indicators, we will compare distributions to situate current findings against earlier assessments; given purposive selection, inferential tests will be used sparingly and interpreted as indicative rather than generalising. Qualitative data from KIIs, FGDs and case-study narratives will be transcribed, translated where needed, and coded in NVivo against a framework built from the six key learning questions: operational meaning of integration, sequencing approaches, challenges and trade-offs, perceptions of SPREAD-introduced structures (CAGs, VSLAs, micro-grants, early warning systems, CMDRR, observatory platforms) versus pre-existing structures (market committees, peace committees, savings groups), integration versus coordination, and contribution to human security and stability. A second inductive coding pass will capture emergent themes, applying Outcome Harvesting logic to substantiate outcome claims with source, description and contribution evidence, and maintaining conflict-sensitive framing and GESI attention throughout. Triangulation will operate at three levels. First, method triangulation: ranking results in SPSS will be read against the NVivo thematic matrix so that each verdict on what must be integrated and what can be coordinated rests on at least two data streams. Second, source triangulation across respondent categories and the four national partner datasets (TUPADO, ICPDO, ROG, ECC), whose harmonised tools permit direct cross-country comparison. Third, temporal triangulation against the baseline, MTR and reflection workshop outputs. Divergent findings will be tabled at the joint review session with the SPREAD team before drafting, ensuring the learning paper's SMART recommendations are defensible at the cross-border validation workshop.
