## SECTION 1: UNDERSTANDING OF THE ASSIGNMENT

### 1.1 The problem the project set out to address

Africa's energy access deficit and its climate obligations pull public finance in two directions. Multilateral development banks, and the African Development Bank in particular, sit at the decision point: their Country Strategy Papers, their Mission 300 Energy Compacts and their investment portfolios determine whether public money entrenches fossil fuel dependency or accelerates decentralised renewable energy. Civil society has legitimate standing in those decisions and substantive evidence to contribute, but its engagement with MDBs has historically been fragmented, episodic and concentrated at the moment of annual meetings rather than sustained across the policy cycle.

Christian Aid's project responded to this with two objectives: strengthening civil society engagement with the AfDB and other MDBs for just and accountable financing of decentralised renewable energy; and increasing divestment of public finance away from fossil fuels alongside uptake of locally led renewable energy systems. The delivery model combined policy research, multi-stakeholder dialogues, capacity building, engagement in AfDB annual meetings and CSO forums, and country and regional advocacy focused on Country Strategy Papers and Mission 300 Energy Compacts, working through KCCWG in Kenya and CISONECC in Malawi.

### 1.2 What this evaluation must resolve

The ToR sets out five intended results, and they are instructive because they are almost entirely *influence* results: AfDB policies incorporating coordinated civil society inputs; decision-makers considering shared multi-stakeholder positions; greater prioritisation of community energy needs in AfDB strategies; AfDB policy improved by project research evidence; and increased media coverage of and demand for DRE.

None of these can be evidenced by counting activities. Each requires the evaluation to establish, first, that a change in an institution's position or output actually occurred, and second, that the project plausibly contributed to it in the presence of other actors and external events. This is the analytical core of the assignment, and it shapes every design decision in Section 3.

We therefore read the assignment as having four tasks:

1. **Establish what changed** in AfDB and MDB policy, strategy, financing priorities, stakeholder positions and public narratives over 2024–2026, using verifiable external evidence rather than project self-report.
2. **Establish the project's contribution** to those changes, with the contribution claim explicitly tested against rival explanations.
3. **Assess delivery** against the logframe indicators and targets, and against the OECD-DAC criteria set out in Section 5.0 — including the efficiency and value-for-money question about engagement modalities, which is unusually concrete in this ToR.
4. **Generate forward-looking learning** that Christian Aid, KCCWG, CISONECC and the wider civil society community can act on, and identify what survives project closure.

### 1.3 Our reading of the evaluation's dual function

Section 2.0 asks the evaluation to serve both accountability and learning. These pull in different directions in practice: accountability rewards a defensible verdict against targets, learning rewards candour about what did not work. We manage this by separating them structurally in the report — findings and conclusions against each DAC criterion form the accountability account, while a distinct learning section addresses what the evidence implies for future MDB engagement — and by using the validation session to test conclusions rather than to soften them.

### 1.4 The attribution problem, stated plainly

Three years is a short window for MDB policy change, and the project was one voice among several — other advocacy coalitions, shifting AfDB leadership priorities, the Mission 300 agenda itself, and wider international pressure on fossil fuel finance were all operating concurrently. An evaluation that claims the project *caused* AfDB policy shifts will not be credible. An evaluation that declines to say anything about influence will not be useful.

Contribution analysis exists precisely for this position. Our approach, set out in Section 3.6, builds a contribution claim for each outcome, assembles the evidence for and against it, actively seeks rival explanations, and reports a graded strength of contribution rather than a binary attribution. Where the evidence does not support a contribution claim, we will say so.

### 1.5 Understanding of scope

The evaluation covers the full implementation period, January 2024 to December 2026, both objectives, and all partners in Kenya and Malawi, together with government departments and other institutions and stakeholders involved. We note that the evaluation is conducted in September–October 2026, before the project's December 2026 close; Section 8.1 addresses the implication.

## SECTION 3: PROPOSED APPROACH AND METHODOLOGY

### 3.1 Design principles

Five principles govern the design.

**Participatory and mixed-methods, as required by Section 6.0.** Qualitative enquiry carries the influence questions; quantitative evidence anchors delivery against logframe targets. Stakeholders are engaged as interpreters of findings, not only as data sources.

**Indicator-anchored, not narrative.** Section 6.0 is explicit that the assessment of effectiveness must be anchored in the logframe's indicators and targets rather than a narrative account of outputs. Our document review builds a performance table for every logframe indicator, with achieved-versus-target values and a documented evidence source for each.

**Contribution, not attribution.** Every influence finding is expressed as a graded contribution claim with its supporting and disconfirming evidence attached.

**Evidence external to the project wherever the claim is about the project.** Where the project claims to have influenced an AfDB document, the evidence must include the AfDB document. We will not accept project reporting as sufficient proof of external influence.

**Transparent about uncertainty.** Where evidence is thin, the report will say so rather than filling the gap with plausible narrative.

### 3.4 Phase 2 — Document review (Weeks 2–4, continuing)

Two distinct review streams.

**Project evidence stream:** project proposal, logframe, Theory of Change, annual technical and donor reports, research outputs and policy briefs, meeting and convening reports, KCCWG and CISONECC partner reports, capacity-building records and participation data, media outputs, and financial and activity records. Output: the logframe indicator performance table and a candidate outcome register.

**External verification stream:** AfDB Country Strategy Papers for Kenya and Malawi, Mission 300 Country Compacts, relevant AfDB energy sector policy and strategy documents, other MDB policy positions where the project engaged, national energy policies and NDCs for both countries, and a structured media corpus covering DRE coverage across the implementation period. Output: independent verification of claimed policy movement, and a baseline against which change is measured.

The separation matters. The second stream is what allows the evaluation to say whether a claimed influence outcome actually appears in the institutional record.

### 3.6 The contribution analysis and outcome harvesting core

This is the methodological centre of the evaluation and we set it out step by step.

**Step 1 — Reconstruct the Theory of Change and surface its critical assumptions.** The project's ToC is restated as an explicit causal chain from activities to the five intended results, with every assumption on which the chain depends made explicit. Section 6.0 requires these assumptions to be tested; they cannot be tested until they are written down.

**Step 2 — Harvest outcome statements.** Working from documents and KIIs, we compile candidate outcome statements in the standard form: *who* changed *what* behaviour, position, policy or practice, *when* and *where*. Outcomes are harvested regardless of whether they are positive, negative, intended or unintended.

**Step 3 — Substantiate each outcome independently.** Each harvested outcome is verified against a source independent of the person who reported it — the AfDB document, the CSP text, the media item, a third-party informant. Outcomes that cannot be substantiated are retained in the register but explicitly marked unsubstantiated and excluded from contribution grading.

**Step 4 — Build the contribution claim.** For each substantiated outcome, we articulate the causal pathway by which project activities are believed to have contributed, and assemble the supporting evidence.

**Step 5 — Actively seek rival explanations.** For each contribution claim we identify what else could account for the outcome: other advocacy coalitions, AfDB internal reform dynamics, the Mission 300 agenda, leadership changes, wider international pressure on fossil fuel finance, or national political events. Interviews with actors outside the project network are used specifically for this purpose. This step is what distinguishes contribution analysis from advocacy narrative, and we treat it as mandatory rather than optional.

**Step 6 — Grade contribution strength.** Each claim receives a transparent grading:

| Grade | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Outcome independently substantiated; a clear causal pathway is documented; multiple independent informants confirm project influence; rival explanations examined and found insufficient to account for the outcome |
| **Moderate** | Outcome substantiated; a plausible pathway is documented with some direct evidence; project influence is one of several credible contributing factors |
| **Weak** | Outcome substantiated but the causal pathway rests largely on project self-report, or rival explanations account for most of the observed change |
| **Not supported** | Outcome unsubstantiated, or evidence indicates the change occurred independently of the project |

**Step 7 — Test the ToC assumptions.** Each critical assumption is assessed as held, partially held or did not hold, with the evidence recorded. Assumptions that did not hold are among the most valuable learning outputs of the evaluation and will be reported as such.

### 3.7 Assessing efficiency and value for money of engagement modalities

Section 3.0 and the Efficiency criterion in Section 5.0 pose an unusually specific question: whether there were more cost-efficient modalities for achieving the same advocacy reach — side events versus standalone missions is the ToR's own example. We treat this as a discrete analytical task rather than a general budget commentary.

For each engagement modality used by the project — AfDB annual meeting participation, CSO forums, side events, standalone missions, national dialogues, research publication, media engagement, learning platform activity — we compile the direct cost from financial records, the reach achieved, and the outcomes traceable to that modality in the outcome register. This produces a comparative modality table showing cost against outcome yield, which is the evidence base for a defensible value-for-money judgement and for a practical recommendation on where future advocacy resource should be concentrated.

The analysis is bounded by the granularity of the project's financial records. Where costs cannot be disaggregated by modality, we will report the limitation rather than estimate.

### 3.8 Gender equality and social inclusion

GESI is assessed as a cross-cutting criterion in its own right, addressing the two questions in Section 5.0: how gender equality and social inclusion were considered in design, advocacy positions and stakeholder engagement; and whether participation and outcome data were disaggregated.

The assessment covers: presence and quality of gender and inclusion analysis in project design; representation of women, youth and off-grid and marginalised community voices in convenings, dialogues and delegations, measured against participation records; whether advocacy positions and research outputs addressed the differentiated energy access needs of women and marginalised groups; and the completeness of the project's own data disaggregation. Where disaggregated data were not collected, that finding is reported directly — the ToR asks the question, and a negative answer is a legitimate and useful finding.

Sampling ensures women respondents across every stakeholder category, and FGDs with women's and youth organisations are conducted separately where this improves candour.

### 3.9 Analysis

Qualitative data are transcribed, coded and analysed in **NVivo** against a coding framework derived from the evaluation matrix, combining deductive codes from the DAC criteria with inductive codes for emergent themes. Quantitative data — logframe performance, participation and disaggregation statistics, cost and reach data — are analysed in **SPSS** and **R** and presented as the indicator performance table and the modality comparison.

Triangulation is applied systematically: no finding enters the report on the basis of a single source. Each finding in the final report carries its evidence base, so that a reader can trace any conclusion back to the data supporting it.

### 3.10 Phase 4 — Validation (Week 7)

A structured validation session with Christian Aid and selected partners, as required by Section 6.0 and Deliverable 3. We circulate the draft report in advance, present emerging findings with their evidence, and work through the contribution claims and their gradings explicitly.

The session is designed to test interpretation, surface evidence the evaluation has missed, and strengthen the practicality of recommendations. It is not a negotiation over findings. Where participants disagree with a conclusion and provide evidence, the conclusion is revised and the revision is recorded; where participants disagree without new evidence, the conclusion stands and the divergent view is reflected in the report. This distinction is stated to participants at the outset and recorded in a validation log annexed to the final report.

### 3.11 Ethics and data protection

In compliance with Section 10.0 and OECD-DAC/UNEG evaluation ethics norms, and given that respondents include AfDB officials, government representatives and media actors:

**Informed consent.** Written or recorded verbal consent is obtained from every KII and FGD participant, following a consent script explaining the evaluation's purpose, how contributions will be used, the voluntary nature of participation, the right to decline any question or withdraw at any point, and the confidentiality regime applying to their data.

**Confidentiality and attribution.** The default is non-attribution. Findings are reported by stakeholder category, not by name or identifying role. Direct attribution occurs only with explicit, separately recorded consent. Where a respondent's role is so distinctive that category-level reporting would still identify them — a specific AfDB position, for example — we aggregate further or generalise the finding.

**Secure data handling.** All primary data are stored encrypted on access-controlled Cortech systems. Recordings and transcripts are held separately from any identifying information, linked only by an anonymised respondent code maintained by the Team Leader. Only named team members have access. Data are retained for the period specified by Christian Aid and securely destroyed thereafter; the retention and destruction schedule is confirmed in the inception report.

**Safeguarding.** Cortech's child safeguarding and PSEA policies apply throughout. All team members are briefed before fieldwork.

**Do no harm.** Given the political sensitivity of fossil fuel finance advocacy, the evaluation avoids placing respondents — particularly government and MDB officials — in a position where recorded views could create professional exposure. Interview settings are chosen accordingly and sensitive material is handled at the aggregate level.

### 4.3 How the eight weeks are made deliverable

The schedule is tight and we have designed against three specific pressure points.

**Kenya and Malawi run concurrently, not sequentially.** A Malawi-based National Consultant leads that country's data collection using common instruments, rather than the core team travelling twice. This is the single largest time saving in the plan.

**Analysis begins during fieldwork, not after it.** Transcription and coding run on a rolling basis from the first interview; outcome harvesting begins in Week 4. The ToR's own timeline overlaps data collection and analysis in Weeks 5–6, and this is how that overlap is absorbed. By the close of fieldwork the outcome register is substantially populated and only contribution testing and synthesis remain.

**The external verification stream starts early and runs independently.** AfDB Country Strategy Papers, Mission 300 Compacts and the media corpus are public documents that do not require fieldwork access. This work begins in Week 2 and proceeds in parallel, so that by the time outcomes are harvested the verification base already exists.

**The validation session is booked at inception.** Scheduling a multi-stakeholder session inside a one-week window is the most common cause of slippage on compressed evaluations. The date is fixed in Week 1 and diarised with all participants at that point.

**Management of the Christian Aid approval gate.** The inception report requires client approval before fieldwork. We will agree a maximum review turnaround at contracting and submit the inception report early in Week 3 to protect the Week 4 fieldwork start. This is a shared dependency and we flag it as such.

## SECTION 8: CLARIFICATIONS AND DECLARED LIMITATIONS

### 8.1 Clarifications requested

1. **Evaluation timing against project close.** The evaluation runs September–October 2026 while the project period extends to December 2026. We propose that the evaluation assesses evidence up to an agreed cut-off, treats the final quarter as planned rather than delivered, and states this in the report. 2. **Duplicated criterion in ToR Section 5.0.** "Sustainability and learning" and "Sustainability" appear as separate criteria with overlapping questions. We have merged them into a single criterion covering both question sets in full, and will confirm at inception.

3. **Availability of financial data granularity.** The Efficiency criterion asks for comparative cost-efficiency across engagement modalities. This requires activity-level cost data. We would welcome early confirmation of the granularity available in project financial records, since it determines how far the modality analysis can be taken.

### 8.2 Declared limitation on AfDB engagement history

The Section 9.0 profile expresses a preference for familiarity with the African Development Bank specifically. We should be straightforward about where we stand. Cortech's institutional experience with multilateral and intergovernmental institutions is substantial — the World Bank, the African Union, IGAD, UNDP, IOM and the EU — and our proposed Malawi National Consultant has delivered assignments for the AfDB directly. We do not, however, claim a portfolio of AfDB-specific policy engagement assignments, and we would rather state that than imply otherwise.

We consider this manageable for an evaluation, as distinct from an advocacy assignment: what the evaluation requires is the ability to read AfDB policy instruments accurately, to secure and conduct credible interviews with AfDB officials, and to assess influence claims rigorously. The first is addressed by the Climate Finance and MDB Policy Specialist, the second depends substantially on Christian Aid's introductions as offered in Section 10.0 of the ToR, and the third is our core competence. We flag the point so the Committee can weigh it with full information.
